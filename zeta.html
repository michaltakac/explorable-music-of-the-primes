Chapter 2

A function that knows every prime

In 1737 Euler found a sum over all whole numbers that equals a product over all primes. In 1859 Riemann let the variable in that identity be a complex number. The tremor from the last chapter turned out to be recorded in the points where his function equals zero.

Euler's product

Pick a number $s$ bigger than 1 and add up $1/n^s$ over every whole number $n$. Euler showed that the same total comes out of multiplying one factor for each prime:

$$\zeta(s)=\sum_{n\ge 1}\frac{1}{n^{s}}=\prod_{p\ \text{prime}}\frac{1}{1-p^{-s}}.$$

With $s=$ 2.00 the full sum is 1.6449. The product over the first 5 primes, up to 11, gives 1.6083. It is short by 0.0366.

The identity is unique factorisation written as a formula. Each factor is a geometric series, $1+p^{-s}+p^{-2s}+\dots$, and when all the series are multiplied out every whole number appears once, because every whole number is a product of primes in one way only.

In plain words

The sum talks about all whole numbers. The product talks only about primes. Because the two are equal, anything learned about ζ is a fact about primes.

Slide $s$ down towards 1. The total grows without limit and the product needs more and more primes to keep up. At $s=1$ the sum is the harmonic series, which is infinite. Euler read that as a proof that the primes never run out, since a product of finitely many factors would be finite.

Complex exponents turn terms into arrows

Riemann's step was to let $s=\sigma+it$ be a complex number. The term $n^{-s}$ is then an arrow in the plane. Its length is $n^{-\sigma}$ and it points in the direction $-t\log n$. So $\sigma$ controls how fast the arrows shrink, and $t$ controls how fast they turn. The sum lays the arrows tip to tail.

At $s=$ 0.70 + 12.00i the value is $\zeta(s)=$ 0.995 − 0.647i, at distance 1.187 from the origin. Here s is inside the critical strip, so the path spirals around ζ(s) without settling.

To the right of $\sigma=1$ the arrows shrink fast enough for the path to settle on a point. Inside the strip $0 < \sigma < 1$ the path never settles. It winds outwards in a growing spiral. The spiral still has a centre, and that centre is $\zeta(s)$. Riemann proved that zeta makes sense for every complex $s$ except $s=1$, through formulas that agree with the sum wherever the sum converges. The process is called analytic continuation.

In plain words

Left of the line σ = 1 the instruction "add up all the terms" stops giving an answer, but the function it described is still there. It is a road that goes on past the edge of the first map.

Zeros

A zero is a value of $s$ where $\zeta(s)=0$. The zeros at $-2,-4,-6,\dots$ are called trivial and play no further part here. Every other zero lies in the critical strip $0 < \sigma < 1$. Riemann computed the first few by hand: $\tfrac12+14.13i$, $\tfrac12+21.02i$, $\tfrac12+25.01i$.

Walking up the critical line

Hold $\sigma$ at $\tfrac12$ and let $t$ climb from 0 to 30.0. The curve on the left has passed through the origin 3 times so far. Each pass is a zero.

Now move the line sideways, to $\sigma=$ 0.50. Up to the same height the curve comes no closer to the origin than 0.003. Off the line $\sigma=\tfrac12$ it keeps missing.

The Riemann hypothesis, stated with zeros

Every zero of $\zeta$ in the critical strip has real part exactly $\tfrac12$.

Every zero computed so far obeys this. Platt and Trudgian verified in 2021 that it holds for all zeros with $t$ up to $3\cdot10^{12}$. A proof has to cover all heights at once.

Zeta also has a mirror symmetry, the functional equation, which relates its value at $s$ to its value at $1-s$. Zeros therefore come in mirrored pairs across the line $\sigma=\tfrac12$, and the hypothesis says each zero is its own mirror image. Chapter 5 shows where the mirror comes from.

The landscape

The surface touches the floor only along the middle line, at the heights from the list above. Nothing in the definition of zeta forces the pits onto a straight line. The next chapter shows what each pit does to the primes.
