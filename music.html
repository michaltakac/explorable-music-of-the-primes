Chapter 3

Zeros as notes

Chapter 1 ended with a tremor in the count of primes. Chapter 2 ended with a list of zeros. Riemann's formula joins them: the tremor is a sum of waves, one wave for each zero.

The explicit formula

Riemann found the formula in 1859, and von Mangoldt proved this cleaner version in 1895. It gives the weighted prime count $\psi(x)$ exactly:

$$\psi(x)=x-\sum_{\rho}\frac{x^{\rho}}{\rho}-\log 2\pi-\tfrac12\log\!\left(1-x^{-2}\right).$$

The sum runs over the zeros $\rho$ of zeta in the critical strip. The last two terms are small corrections. Everything interesting is in the sum.

With 10 zeros the formula is the line plus 10 waves. Each added zero bends the curve a little closer to the stairs. At $x=$ 60 the formula gives 58.04 and the true value is 57.53.

Start from zero zeros: the formula is the straight line. Add the first zero and the line starts to ripple. With a few dozen, steps appear at 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9, 11: the primes and their powers, in the right places, built from numbers that look unrelated to them.

In plain words

The prime staircase is a sound. The straight line is its steady hum, and each zero is one pure note. Play every note together and you hear exactly where the primes are.

One zero is one wave

Zeros come in pairs $\tfrac12\pm i\gamma$. A pair contributes the term

$$-\frac{x^{\rho}}{\rho}-\frac{x^{\bar\rho}}{\bar\rho}=-\frac{2\sqrt{x}}{|\rho|}\,\cos\!\big(\gamma\log x-\varphi\big),$$

where $\varphi$ is a fixed phase shift. Measured against $\log x$ this is a pure wave. Its pitch is the height $\gamma$ of the zero, and its size grows like $\sqrt{x}$.

Zero number 1 sits at height $\gamma=$ 14.135. Its wave completes one cycle each time $x$ is multiplied by 1.560. Higher zeros give faster waves with smaller size, since the size is divided by $|\rho|\approx\gamma$.

The hypothesis sets the volume

The size of a wave is $x^{\beta}$, where $\beta$ is the real part of its zero. For a zero on the critical line that is $\sqrt{x}$. A zero off the line, with $\beta>\tfrac12$, would give a wave that grows faster than all the others and eventually drowns them out.

The graph below adds a made-up pair of zeros at height 10: one at real part 0.50 and its mirror image at 0.50. Zeta has no zero there. This is what the prime count would look like if it did.

At $x=100{,}000$ the made-up notes alone swing by about 126 either way, against $\sqrt{x}\approx316$. With $\beta=\tfrac12$ they stay in proportion to the band. Push $\beta$ up and they break out of it.

This is the content of the Riemann hypothesis for primes. If all zeros have real part $\tfrac12$, every wave has size $\sqrt{x}$ and the tremor is as small as chapter 1 hoped. If the largest real part of any zero is $\Theta$, the tremor reaches size $x^{\Theta}$.

In plain words

The hypothesis says no instrument in the orchestra is louder than the rest. One loud instrument would be one zero off the line, and the primes would visibly lurch to its rhythm.

Reading the primes back out of the zeros

The formula also runs backwards. Add up the plain cosines $\cos(\gamma u)$ over the first 100 zeros, flip the sign and rescale. The sum stays near 0 except at special values of $u$, where it spikes. The spikes sit at $u=\log 2,\ \log 3,\ \log 4,\ \log 5,\ \log 7,\dots$: the logarithms of the prime powers.

So the zeros know the primes, and the primes know the zeros. A statement about one side can be translated into a statement about the other. A formula that performs the translation for a whole class of test functions at once is called an explicit formula, or in a more structural setting a trace formula. The manuscript this site follows is built around one.

Zeta is the first of a large family of functions with the same structure. The claim in the manuscript covers a whole branch of that family, so the family comes next.
