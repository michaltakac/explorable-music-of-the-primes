The music of the primes

Alessandro Maria Masullo's manuscript of this title runs to 940 pages and sets out a proof of the generalized Riemann hypothesis for a large family of L-functions. This guide builds up the ideas behind it in seven short chapters, with figures you can change by hand.

The curve above uses zeros. Drag that number. Each zero adds one wave, and with enough waves the curve finds every prime. The Riemann hypothesis is a statement about how loud those waves are allowed to be. Chapter 3 explains the picture.

Anything with a dotted amber underline can be dragged sideways, and so can every amber point inside a figure. The arrow keys work too. The text around a figure recomputes as you move things.

The manuscript's status

The PDF this guide is based on is dated 20 November 2026. It is a draft that has not been refereed. In its acknowledgements the author describes the mathematics as complete and the writing as unfinished, and the chapter that should state and prove the theorem about zeros is still a placeholder. Until specialists have checked the argument, the generalized Riemann hypothesis remains an open problem with a new claimed proof.

Chapters 1 to 6 of this guide cover mathematics that is long established. Chapter 7 describes the manuscript's strategy as its author presents it. The status page lists what the current text does and does not contain.

    About this guide

    The figures are computed in your browser as you read: primes by a sieve, values of zeta and of Dirichlet L-functions by Euler-Maclaurin summation, and zeros from a table computed to 15 digits with the mpmath library. They are drawn with MathBox. The format follows Bret Victor's idea of an explorable explanation, in which the reader can test what the author says.

    The guide was prepared with an AI model, Claude, from a reading of the full PDF. It is not affiliated with the manuscript's author. Mistakes in the guide belong to the guide.
