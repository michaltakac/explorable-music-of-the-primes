Chapter 4

The whole family of L-functions

Zeta counts all primes together. Sort the primes into classes first, and each way of sorting produces its own function with its own zeros. The generalized Riemann hypothesis says that every one of these functions keeps its zeros on the same line.

A race between two teams

Every odd prime leaves remainder 1 or 3 when divided by 4. Call them team 1 (5, 13, 17, 29, ...) and team 3 (3, 7, 11, 19, ...). Dirichlet proved in 1837 that both teams are infinite and, in the long run, the same size. Up to , team 3 has primes and team 1 has .

To follow the race, tag every whole number with a sign: $\chi(n)=+1$ if $n$ leaves remainder 1, $\chi(n)=-1$ if it leaves remainder 3, and $\chi(n)=0$ if $n$ is even. A tagging rule like this, one that respects multiplication, is called a character. The weighted score

$$\psi(x,\chi)=\sum_{p^k\le x}\chi(p^k)\log p$$

goes up when team 1 scores and down when team 3 scores.

The score has no smooth main term, because neither team wins in the long run. All that is left is tremor, and the tremor is again a sum of waves. The waves come from the zeros of a new function attached to the character:

$$L(s,\chi)=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\chi(n)}{n^{s}}=\prod_{p}\frac{1}{1-\chi(p)\,p^{-s}}.$$

With pairs of its zeros the formula already follows the race. The score spends most of its time below zero: team 3 usually leads. Chebyshev noticed this in 1853. The explanation is the size of the waves, which is $\sqrt{x}$ if the zeros of $L(s,\chi)$ have real part $\tfrac12$.

Characters and their zeros

The generalized Riemann hypothesis for Dirichlet characters

For every character $\chi$, every zero of $L(s,\chi)$ in the strip $0 < \sigma < 1$ has real part $\tfrac12$.

In plain words

Each character is a different way of listening to the primes. The hypothesis says that every way of listening hears the same thing: noise of the smallest possible size.

Other number systems

The whole numbers are one arithmetic among many. Adjoin $i=\sqrt{-1}$ and you get the Gaussian integers $a+bi$, which have primes of their own. A number field is any system built from the rationals by adjoining roots of polynomial equations in this way.

Inside radius there are Gaussian primes. The race from the top of this chapter decides their shape: a prime from team 1 splits into two Gaussian primes, and a prime from team 3 stays whole. The zeta function of the Gaussian integers reflects that. It is the product $\zeta(s)\,L(s,\chi)$ with $\chi$ the character mod 4.

Primes in a number field can be tagged too. For Gaussian primes one can use the direction in which the prime points, $\chi(a+bi)=\big(\tfrac{a+bi}{|a+bi|}\big)^{4}$. Tags of this kind are called Hecke characters, after Erich Hecke, who showed around 1920 that their L-functions have the same mirror symmetry and the same kind of zeros as zeta. Dirichlet characters are the Hecke characters of the rational numbers that take finitely many values.

The scope of the claim

The manuscript states its goal for this entire branch of the family:

The statement the manuscript sets out to prove

Let $F$ be any number field and $\chi$ any unitary Hecke character of $F$. Then every zero of the completed L-function $L(s,\chi)$ has real part $\tfrac12$.

This contains the Riemann hypothesis ($F$ the rationals, $\chi$ trivial), the hypothesis for all Dirichlet L-functions, and the hypothesis for the zeta function of every number field. It does not contain the L-functions of higher rank, such as those attached to elliptic curves over the rationals or to modular forms. The manuscript's last section says that the higher-rank version is the subject of ongoing work.
