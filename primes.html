Chapter 1

Counting primes

A prime is a whole number bigger than 1 that only 1 and itself divide: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13. No formula hands them out one after another. Ask a different question, how many primes there are below a given size, and a regular shape shows up.

A staircase

Walk along the number line and climb one step each time you pass a prime. Your height when you reach $x$ is written $\pi(x)$, the number of primes up to $x$.

Up to 100 there are 25 primes. Gauss, working from printed tables as a teenager, guessed that near a number $x$ about one number in $\log x$ is prime. Adding up those odds from 2 to $x$ gives the smooth curve

$$\mathrm{Li}(x)=\int_2^x \frac{dt}{\log t},$$

which predicts 29.1. The guess is off by 4.1, which is 16.3% of the count.

In plain words

Primes thin out as numbers grow, and they thin out at a known rate. Near a million about one number in 14 is prime. Near a billion it is about one in 21.

Drag the limit up towards 100,000. The steps shrink until the staircase and the curve are hard to tell apart. That is the prime number theorem, proved in 1896 by Hadamard and by de la Vallée Poussin: the ratio $\pi(x)/\mathrm{Li}(x)$ tends to 1.

Weighing each prime

The bookkeeping gets cleaner when every prime $p$ counts for $\log p$ instead of 1, and its powers $p^2, p^3, \dots$ count for $\log p$ as well. The weighted count is Chebyshev's function

$$\psi(x)=\sum_{p^k\le x}\log p .$$

With this weighting Gauss's curve becomes the straight line $y=x$, and the prime number theorem says $\psi(x)$ hugs that line.

The tremor

Subtract the line and look at what is left over. The leftover $\psi(x)-x$ never settles. It swings above and below zero for as far as anyone has computed, and it is known to keep changing sign forever.

Up to 1,000 the largest miss is 16.3, while $\sqrt{x}$ at the right-hand edge is 31.6. The tremor stays inside the band or close to it for as far as this graph goes.

How large can the tremor get in the long run? That question is the Riemann hypothesis, in the form most useful for counting primes:

The Riemann hypothesis, stated with primes

There is a constant $C$ such that $|\psi(x)-x|\le C\,\sqrt{x}\,(\log x)^2$ for every $x\ge 2$.

Nobody has proved a bound anywhere near this. The best proven bounds are only slightly better than $x$ itself. Square-root size is also the smallest tremor one could hope for: it is what a fair coin produces when it decides, number by number, who is prime.

In plain words

The primes follow a smooth law with noise on top. The Riemann hypothesis says the noise is as small as noise can be.

The tremor is a sum of pure waves, and each wave comes from a single point in the plane where a certain function equals zero. The next chapter builds that function.
