Chapter 6

Two ways to count the same thing

A trace formula computes one number in two ways and sets the answers equal. One way sees primes and the other sees zeros. This chapter works through the smallest example by hand. It is the same toy example the manuscript uses to explain its method.

The trace of a smear

Take a list of numbers and a rule that replaces each entry by a weighted mix of all the entries. That rule is a matrix. Its trace is the sum of the weights on the diagonal: how much of each entry stays where it was. Linear algebra says the trace also equals the sum of the eigenvalues, the factors by which the matrix stretches its own preferred directions.

The same holds for functions in place of lists. A smear replaces the value at each point by a weighted average of values elsewhere. The weights form a kernel $k(x,y)$, and the trace is the total of $k(x,x)$ along the diagonal.

In plain words

The trace asks how much of everything is still where it started. You can check place by place, or you can add up how much each natural vibration of the system was amplified. Both give the same number.

A loop

Wind the positive numbers into a loop by declaring $x$ and $\gamma x$ to be the same point, for a fixed $\gamma>1$. In the coordinate $u=\log x$ this is a circle of circumference $L=\log\gamma$. The smear is built from a bump $\varphi$: it pushes the value at every point forward around the loop, spread out according to the shape of the bump.

Counting by places. A point keeps part of its own value only if the smear carries it all the way around and back home, once, twice, or any whole number of times. So the diagonal weight is the bump evaluated at the closing points $u=0,\pm L,\pm2L,\dots$, and

$$\text{trace}=L\cdot\sum_{k}\varphi(kL).$$

Counting by modes. The natural vibrations of a loop are the pure waves that fit around it a whole number $m$ of times. The smear multiplies wave number $m$ by a number $\widehat\varphi(m)$, the strength of that frequency in the bump. So

$$\text{trace}=\sum_{m}\widehat\varphi(m).$$

The equality of the two counts is the Poisson summation formula from the last chapter. The vanishing is the fact the manuscript leans on. Its introduction states it for this example: when the bump is supported strictly inside $(1,\gamma)$, the trace on the loop is zero.

Every prime is a loop

Inside the idele class group, each prime $p$ marks out a loop of exactly this kind, with $\gamma=p$ and length $\log p$. In a number field, each prime ideal gives a loop whose length is the logarithm of its norm. Christopher Deninger proposed in the 1990s that the primes ought to be the closed orbits of some flow, with these lengths. Chapter 8 of the manuscript locates such loops as the places where pieces of its function spaces are concentrated. The author adds that this picture organises the calculation and is not logically needed for it.

The explicit formula is a trace formula

André Weil wrote the explicit formula in 1952 in a form that looks just like the two counts above. For a test function $\varphi$ and its Mellin transform $\widehat\varphi$:

$$\sum_{\text{places } v}\big(\text{term for } v\big)(\varphi)\;=\;\widehat\varphi(\tfrac12)+\widehat\varphi(-\tfrac12)-\sum_{\rho}\widehat\varphi\big(\rho-\tfrac12\big).$$

On the left, one term for each prime and one for each ordinary ruler. On the right, one term for each zero $\rho$, plus two terms that come from the pole of zeta. The left side is a count by places and the right side is a count by modes. Weil proved the formula by complex analysis. He did not have an operator whose trace it computes.

Alain Connes proposed in 1998 that there is one: the smear by $\varphi$, acting on functions on the idele class group. He proved the count by places when only finitely many primes are involved, and showed that a global version in his Hilbert space setting would be equivalent to the Riemann hypothesis. Ralf Meyer proved a global trace formula in 2005 on a much larger space of functions. In Meyer's space every zero contributes, wherever it lies, so his formula reproduces Weil's and carries no information about where the zeros are.

The manuscript aims for a trace formula in a setting between those two. The last chapter explains how.
